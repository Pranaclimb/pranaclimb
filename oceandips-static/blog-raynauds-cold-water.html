Cold Water & Science

Raynaud's, Cold-Water Pain and What Happens When You Enter the Ocean

By Annie Anderson · 15 min read

Cold water can feel exhilarating, energising, and deeply calming — but the first few seconds can also feel intense.

Some people experience gasping, tingling, aching, burning, or pain in their hands and feet. For others, the fingers may turn white, blue, and then red.

These reactions can be alarming, especially if you are new to cold-water swimming. Understanding what your body is doing can make the experience feel far less frightening and help you recognise the difference between a normal response to cold and Raynaud's phenomenon.

Your Body's Survival Response

The moment you enter cold water, your body goes into survival mode. Your brain immediately begins protecting your vital organs by reducing heat loss. One of the fastest ways it does this is by narrowing the blood vessels in your hands, feet, arms and legs. This process is called vasoconstriction. Blood is redirected away from your fingers and toes towards your heart, lungs and brain to preserve your core temperature. This is completely normal.

🌊 What Happens to Your Body When You Enter Cold Water?

When you first enter cold water, your body experiences what scientists call the cold shock response — an automatic survival reaction triggered by the rapid cooling of the skin.

Within the first few seconds:

  • 🫁 Your breathing speeds up
  • ❤️ Your heart rate rises
  • 🩸 Blood vessels in the skin narrow (vasoconstriction) to limit heat loss and prioritise vital organs
  • ⚡ Stress hormones are released
  • 👀 You become more alert

You may experience:

  • An involuntary gasp
  • Rapid or difficult-to-control breathing
  • Tingling or stinging skin
  • A racing heart
  • Tension through the chest and shoulders
  • A sudden feeling of panic or urgency

This can feel alarming, but it is a recognised physiological response to sudden cold-water immersion. The strongest effects often occur during the first minute or so. This is why it is important to enter slowly, remain within your depth, and regain control of your breathing before beginning to swim.

As you settle:

  • 🫁 Breathing may begin to slow
  • ❤️ Heart rate may become steadier
  • 🧠 The sense of panic often reduces
  • 🌊 The water can begin to feel more manageable

Repeated, carefully managed exposure may reduce the magnitude of the initial cold shock response. This adaptation is known as cold habituation. It does not make cold water risk-free, but many regular swimmers find that the initial gasp and breathing response become easier to manage over time.

🫁 Why Breathing Matters

The greatest immediate challenge is often not the sensation of cold itself. It is the urge to gasp and breathe rapidly. Rapid breathing can increase the feeling of panic and make it harder to think clearly. Trying to swim immediately can add physical effort before breathing control has returned.

At Ocean Dips, the first goal is therefore simple: enter slowly. Pause. Regain your breath. Swim only when you feel in control.

Focusing on exhales can help you settle without encouraging excessively deep or rapid breathing. This is very different from hyperventilation or breath-hold practice, neither of which should be performed immediately before entering the water or while in it.

🩸 What Happens to Your Circulation?

Once you enter cold water, your body begins protecting its core. Blood vessels near the surface of the skin and in the arms and legs narrow (peripheral vasoconstriction), reducing heat loss and redirecting more blood towards the heart, lungs, brain, and other vital organs.

You may notice:

  • ❄️ Cold fingers and toes
  • ❄️ Pale skin
  • ❄️ Stiffness or reduced dexterity
  • ❄️ Aching in the hands and feet

These can be normal protective responses to cold. However, the reduction in blood flow can also contribute to discomfort — particularly in the fingers and toes, which contain many temperature-sensitive nerve endings.

🔥 Why Can Cold Water Feel Painful?

The pain of entering cold water is usually caused by a combination of rapid cooling, nerve activation, and reduced circulation.

Stinging, stabbing and pins and needles

Cold-sensitive pain receptors (nociceptors) in the skin respond rapidly when the temperature falls. The rate of temperature change is just as important as the absolute temperature — the faster the temperature falls, the more intense the nociceptive signalling becomes. The brain may interpret these signals as sharp stinging, pinpricks, or needle-like pain.

Aching and throbbing

As blood vessels constrict, blood flow and oxygen delivery to the hands and feet reduce. This can create a deeper ache or throbbing sensation, particularly in the fingers and toes.

Burning sensations

Cold does not always feel simply "cold." Overstimulated sensory nerves can sometimes create a paradoxical burning sensation.

Numbness

As nerves and tissues continue to cool, sensation and dexterity may decrease. Numbness is not a sign to stay in longer — it can reduce your ability to judge how cold you are and make tasks such as dressing, handling equipment, or leaving the water more difficult.

🔄 The Hunting Reaction

The body may occasionally reopen blood vessels in the fingers or toes for short periods, allowing warmer blood to return. This is often called the cold-induced vasodilation response or Lewis hunting reaction. It can create alternating sensations of warmth, throbbing, pressure, aching, and temporary relief. However, this response varies considerably between people and should not be relied upon as protection from excessive cooling.

❄️ What Is Raynaud's?

Raynaud's (pronounced ray-NOSE) is a condition in which the small blood vessels — most commonly in the fingers and toes — react excessively to cold or emotional stress. The nose, lips and ears can be affected too. During an episode, the vessels may go into a pronounced spasm and significantly restrict blood flow.

The affected areas may:

🤍 Turn white as blood flow reduces 💙 Turn blue as oxygen falls ❤️ Become red as circulation returns

Not everyone experiences every colour stage. Other symptoms can include pain, numbness, tingling, pins and needles, throbbing, burning, and reduced hand or foot function. The red, painful, or throbbing stage often appears during rewarming, when fresh blood returns.

According to the NHS, there are over 10 million people living with Raynaud's in the UK, and it affects around 20% of the adult population worldwide.

🌊 How Is Raynaud's Different from a Normal Cold Response?

During normal cold-water immersion, blood vessels narrow to conserve heat, but some blood flow usually continues. With Raynaud's, the response is more extreme — the vessels can go into a powerful temporary spasm, causing a much greater reduction in circulation.

Normal vasoconstriction is the body turning the tap down. Raynaud's is the body temporarily clamping the tap almost shut.

Raynaud's is not simply "poor circulation," and the body is not broken. It is an exaggerated vascular response to cold or stress — rather like an overprotective thermostat.

Primary Raynaud's

Primary Raynaud's is the more common form. It is not caused by another identified medical condition and is often relatively mild.

Secondary Raynaud's

Secondary Raynaud's is associated with another condition, including some autoimmune or connective-tissue diseases. It may be more severe and is more likely to cause persistent pain, sores, ulcers, or tissue damage.

Medical advice is particularly important when:

  • Symptoms begin later in life
  • Only one side is affected
  • Pain is severe
  • Episodes are worsening
  • Symptoms persist after rewarming
  • Sores, ulcers, infection, or tissue damage appear
  • Raynaud's is linked to another health condition

❤️ Can Cold-Water Swimming Improve Raynaud's?

Some people report that gradual, regular cold exposure makes their symptoms feel less severe or improves their confidence around cold. Possible explanations may include:

  • ✅ Cold habituation
  • ✅ Reduced anxiety around immersion
  • ✅ Improved familiarity with the body's response
  • ✅ Better preparation and rewarming habits
  • ✅ Greater breathing control

However, this does not mean cold water is a proven treatment for Raynaud's. Cold is also one of the main triggers of a Raynaud's episode, and scientific evidence that repeated cold-water immersion improves the condition remains limited. Some people tolerate carefully managed dips well. Others find that cold water consistently worsens their symptoms. The aim should never be to "push through" severe pain, numbness, or colour change in the hope of forcing adaptation.

🧜‍♀️ Ocean Dips Tips for People with Raynaud's

Before entering

  • 🧤 Stay warm until the last possible moment
  • 🧦 Consider thermal neoprene socks
  • 🧤 Consider specialised neoprene gloves
  • 🧢 Wear a brightly coloured swim cap or thermal cap
  • 🚶 Use gentle movement to warm the body
  • 🫁 Settle your breathing before entry
  • 🌊 Enter gradually rather than jumping in
  • 💧 Arrive well hydrated
  • ☕ Avoid excessive caffeine if it worsens your symptoms

Keeping the torso warm is important. When the body feels threatened by cold, it may restrict blood flow to the hands and feet even more strongly.

During the dip

  • Stay close to a safe exit
  • Keep the first immersion short
  • Regain breathing control before swimming
  • Remain aware of pain, sensation, and hand function
  • Leave the water if pain becomes severe or numbness increases
  • Do not use duration as a measure of success
  • Never swim alone

For some people, keeping the hands out of the water initially or wearing neoprene gloves makes the experience considerably more comfortable.

After the dip

  • 🧥 Dry and dress immediately
  • 👕 Begin with warm layers around the torso
  • 🧢 Put on a hat
  • 🧤 Add dry gloves
  • 🧦 Replace wet socks or footwear
  • 🚶 Walk gently to generate warmth
  • ☕ Have a warm non-alcoholic drink
  • 🌬️ Keep breathing calm and unforced

Warm the body gradually. Very hot water applied immediately to severely cold or numb hands can be painful. Do not rub numb hands or feet aggressively, and do not place them directly against very hot surfaces.

🧠 Pain Is Information

Cold-water discomfort should not be treated as a test of courage. Mild stinging at entry may settle. Severe pain, increasing numbness, loss of dexterity, persistent colour change, dizziness, confusion, or uncontrollable shivering are signals to leave the water and rewarm.

At Ocean Dips, we do not encourage people to compete with the cold or prove how long they can remain immersed. The aim is to remain attentive enough to respond intelligently.

🌊 The Ocean Dips View

We do not enter cold water to prove how tough we are.

We enter to practise a valuable skill: feeling a stress response arise — and learning how to breathe, adapt, and recover.

Every calm breath can become a reminder:

❤️ "I can listen to my body."
🫁 "I can regulate my breathing."
🌊 "I can respond without panic."

But regulation does not mean ignoring warning signs. Sometimes the most resilient decision is to slow down, shorten the dip, protect the hands and feet, or stay out of the water entirely.

Cold-water confidence is not measured by suffering. It is measured by awareness, preparation, and the ability to work with your body rather than against it.

Raynaud's, Cold Water & Ocean Dips

Why do my hands hurt in cold water?

If you've ever stepped into the sea and felt an intense aching, burning, or stabbing pain in your hands or feet, you're not alone. Many people experience discomfort when entering cold water. For some, it's simply part of the body's normal response to the cold. For others, it may be a sign of Raynaud's phenomenon, a common condition affecting more than 10 million people in the UK.

At Ocean Dips, we often meet people who ask: "Is this normal?" The answer is usually yes — but it's important to understand the difference between a normal cold response and Raynaud's.

Why Does It Hurt?

Stabbing or Pins and Needles — Cold-sensitive nerve endings fire rapidly, sending distress signals to your brain. The sensation is often described as sharp needles or stabbing pain.

Deep Aching — Reduced blood flow means less oxygen reaches your fingers and toes. The combination of cooling tissues and reduced circulation produces a deep aching sensation.

Burning — Interestingly, very cold nerves sometimes confuse the brain. Instead of simply reporting "cold", they generate signals that feel like burning.

Numbness — Eventually, the nerves become so cold that they stop transmitting signals efficiently. Your fingers or toes begin to feel numb.

The Hunting Reaction

Your body doesn't completely abandon your fingers. Every few minutes it may briefly relax the blood vessels, allowing warm blood to return — called the Lewis Hunting Reaction. You may notice throbbing, warmth, pressure, and temporary relief before the vessels narrow again. This cycling helps protect your tissues from becoming too cold.

Can You Still Enjoy Cold Water?

In many cases, yes. Many people with mild Primary Raynaud's continue to enjoy sea swimming. The key is to build up gradually and listen to your body. At Ocean Dips we always recommend:

  • 🧤 Thermal neoprene gloves
  • 🧦 Thermal neoprene socks
  • 🩱 A swim cap
  • 🧘 Enter the water slowly
  • 🌬️ Focus on slow, controlled breathing
  • 🏊 Keep swims short
  • 🚶 Warm up naturally afterwards

Everyone responds differently, so it's important to progress at your own pace.

Our Approach at Ocean Dips

At Ocean Dips, we never encourage people to "push through" pain. Cold water should always be approached with respect. Every guided experience is tailored to the individual, allowing plenty of time to settle the breath, adapt gradually, and enjoy the remarkable benefits of the ocean safely.

For many people, the greatest achievement isn't swimming further — it's simply learning to relax, breathe, and feel calm in cold water.
